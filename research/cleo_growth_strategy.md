## Executive Summary

Cleo is not constrained by demand. Berlin's ageing population guarantees a growing client pipeline for the foreseeable future. The constraint is operational capacity — specifically, how many clients Cleo's existing 85 caregivers can serve within a given working day.

The three AI use cases identified in this engagement — care documentation, tour planning optimisation, and staff churn prediction — collectively unlock the equivalent of 12 to 16 additional full-time caregiver positions from Cleo's existing workforce. Without a single new hire, that translates into capacity for 40 to 60 additional clients and an estimated €190,000 to €300,000 in additional annual revenue under the base case scenario.

This report answers the question: what does that mean for Cleo's growth over the next five years, and what are the realistic limits of that growth?

---

## 1. The Market Opportunity

### Berlin's care demand is growing faster than supply

Berlin's Senatsverwaltung für Gesundheit projects that the number of people requiring care in the city will grow by at least 12% in ambulatory settings by 2030. At year-end 2023, nearly 212,000 Berliners were already receiving care insurance benefits — with 87% of them being cared for at home. That figure will continue to rise as the 80+ cohort grows and Berlin's policy framework continues to favour home-based care over residential placement.

Nationally, Germany faces a projected shortfall of up to 500,000 care workers by 2034. The ambulatory care sector is growing at a CAGR of 5.8% through 2035. Demand will structurally outpace supply — meaning any provider that can serve more clients with the same workforce has a durable competitive advantage.

**For Clara:** The clients will be there. The strategic question is whether Cleo has the operational capacity to serve them — and that is exactly what the AI investment addresses.

## 2. How AI Unlocks Growth Capacity

The three use cases work as a compounding system, not three isolated tools. Think of Cleo's current operational capacity like a bucket with three leaks — time lost to documentation, time lost to inefficient routes, and capacity lost every time an experienced caregiver exits. Each AI phase plugs one of those leaks.

### Phase 1 — Care Documentation: recovering daily time

Every caregiver spends an estimated 20–30% of their working time on documentation. A voice AI tool (such as voize, already deployed in 2,000+ German care facilities) reduces that burden by 27% on average, based on the Charité Berlin PYSA study. Across 85 caregivers, that recovered time is the equivalent of roughly 10–12 additional hours of productive care capacity per day — time that can be redirected to more client visits.

**Growth impact:** Supports serving an additional 15–25 clients from existing headcount, with no new infrastructure required.

### Phase 2 — Tour Planning Optimisation: recovering travel time

Unoptimised routes are invisible lost capacity. Comparable European home care operators have achieved 20–35% reductions in caregiver travel time through AI route optimisation. For Cleo's 85 caregivers operating across Berlin's districts, that translates into the equivalent of 5–8 additional caregiver hours per day freed from windshield time.

**Growth impact:** Supports serving an additional 15–25 clients — but only if Cleo has a client waitlist to fill the freed capacity. Clara must confirm current utilisation rate before this figure can be confirmed.

### Phase 3 — Staff Churn Prediction: recovering lost experience

At 35% annual turnover, Cleo loses approximately 30 caregivers per year. Each exit costs an estimated €3,860 in recruitment and onboarding — and more importantly, removes experienced capacity that takes months to rebuild. A churn prediction model that reduces exits by 50% retains the equivalent of 15 experienced caregivers per year, directly protecting the capacity gains built in Phases 1 and 2.

**Growth impact:** Not a direct client capacity gain, but a capacity stabiliser. Without churn reduction, gains from Phases 1 and 2 partially erode each time an experienced caregiver exits.

## 4. Growth Ceilings — What Limits Scale

This is the most important section of this report for strategic planning. The AI investment unlocks capacity, but it does not remove all constraints on growth. Clara should be aware of two hard ceilings and one soft ceiling.

### Hard ceiling 1: SGB XI reimbursement structure

Cleo cannot charge more per visit — rates are fixed by Krankenkasse agreements. Every euro of revenue growth must come from volume: more clients served, more visits completed. This means growth is fundamentally gated by whether Cleo has clients to fill freed capacity. The AI investment only generates its full financial return if demand is present. Confirming a client waitlist is the single most important step before committing to Phase 2.

### Hard ceiling 2: caregiver supply in Berlin

AI can free up to 30–35% additional capacity from the existing workforce. Beyond that threshold, further growth requires hiring. In a market where Germany is projected to face a shortfall of 500,000 care workers by 2034, recruitment is difficult and expensive. Cleo's growth strategy beyond a 35–40% client increase must include an active recruitment and training programme — the AI investment supports this by making Cleo a more attractive employer (less burnout, better schedules), but does not replace the need to hire.

### Soft ceiling: three-district operating footprint

Cleo currently operates in three Berlin Bezirke. Expanding to additional districts is the most realistic path to step-change growth once AI has stabilised operations internally. Berlin has twelve Bezirke, and the ambulatory care market is highly fragmented — most providers are small, single-district operators. A well-run, AI-optimised Cleo entering two or three new districts would be entering markets with structural demand and limited competition from operationally sophisticated providers.

## 5. The Geographic Expansion Opportunity

District expansion is not a Phase 1 or Phase 2 consideration — it is a Phase 3 and beyond horizon. But it is worth flagging now as the strategic destination that the AI investment is building toward.

The case for expansion rests on three facts:

First, Berlin's care demand is unevenly distributed. Districts with higher concentrations of the 75+ cohort — including Spandau, Reinickendorf, and Lichtenberg — have growing unmet ambulatory care demand and fewer established mid-sized providers than Cleo's current operating districts.

Second, tour planning AI is specifically built for geographic complexity. The Vehicle Routing Problem that AI optimises becomes more valuable, not less, as Cleo's footprint grows. Manual scheduling across five or six districts is operationally impractical; AI-assisted scheduling is not.

Third, Cleo's brand and quality rating are portable. A strong MD inspection rating — which AI documentation directly supports — is a reputational asset that transfers to new districts and accelerates client acquisition.

**A secondary benefit of district expansion: revenue mix.** Districts like Charlottenburg-Wilmersdorf and Steglitz-Zehlendorf have a higher concentration of private-pay demand than Cleo's current footprint. Expanding into these districts would, as a side effect, gradually increase Cleo's share of private-pay revenue — improving margins without requiring a deliberate strategic pivot away from SGB XI. This should not be read as a path to reducing reliance on the Pflegeversicherung; the German market structure makes a full pivot to private pay unrealistic. It is a margin improvement that comes "for free" with the expansion already recommended for capacity reasons.

**The realistic expansion timeline:** If Phase 1 deploys in Q3 2026 and Phase 2 in Q1 2027, Cleo could credibly begin scoping a fourth district pilot in late 2027, with a full expansion programme in 2028–2029. By 2030, a four-to-five district Cleo could be serving 600–700 clients — roughly double its current scale — while operating at higher efficiency than it does today at 320.

## 7. Summary Recommendation

Cleo is in a structurally advantaged position. It operates in a growing market, has an established quality reputation, and faces a constraint — operational capacity — that is directly solvable through AI investment. The three-phase programme recommended in the business case is not just a cost-saving exercise. It is the foundation of a growth strategy that could double Cleo's client base by 2030 while making the organisation more resilient, more compliant, and more attractive to the caregivers it depends on.

The growth is not guaranteed — it depends on demand being present, caregivers being retained, and district expansion being executed well. But the direction is clear, the market data supports it, and the tools to pursue it now exist and are proven in the German care sector.

The question is not whether to invest. It is how quickly Cleo can move.
